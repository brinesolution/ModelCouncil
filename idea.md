# 1. Original Idea

The project began with a broad idea around **AI agents analytics and population simulation**: create many simulated individuals, allow them to hold opinions, and analyze how those opinions develop across a population.

The concept was not intended to be another chatbot. The interesting question was whether a synthetic population could be modeled as a set of different people with different characteristics and then observed as those people react to an idea.

The initial target was gradually narrowed to a concrete portfolio problem:

> **Consumer behaviour and product analysis.**

A company, founder, researcher, student, or product team should be able to submit a product idea or product pitch and observe how a heterogeneous synthetic population reacts.

The system should produce more than a single "people liked it" number. It should show which kinds of people responded positively or negatively, why, what they objected to, how price and trust matter, and how social discussion changes the market response.

# 4. Randomness Without Nonsense

Pure independent random sampling would produce unrealistic consumers, so the idea was refined to include:

- conditional distributions;
- trait correlations;
- compatibility constraints;
- archetypes;
- random variation around archetypes.

Example dependency chain:

```text
Age
 ↓
Education
 ↓
Occupation
 ↓
Income
 ↓
Disposable income
 ↓
Price sensitivity
```

Examples of softer correlations:

```text
higher income -> usually lower price sensitivity
high novelty seeking -> usually greater early adoption
high stubbornness -> usually lower susceptibility
high privacy concern -> potentially lower AI-product trust
```

These are tendencies, not absolute stereotypes. Random variation remains important.

A possible generation pattern is:

```text
Archetype
 + demographic constraints
 + probabilistic traits
 + compatibility rules
 + random variation
 = Consumer Agent
```

# 5. Consumers Become Social Agents

The next major extension was to move beyond isolated survey responses.

In real markets people talk to:

- friends;
- colleagues;
- family;
- communities;
- online contacts;
- reviewers;
- influencers.

Therefore synthetic consumers should affect one another.

Every consumer is represented as a normalized trait vector. Similarity is calculated using weighted distance. K-nearest neighbours create a candidate social neighbourhood.

Important refinement:

> **KNN does not mean every consumer talks to all K neighbours every round.**

K only determines the candidate social neighbourhood. A conversation scheduler chooses a small number of actual contacts.

This prevents the simulation from degenerating into simple neighbour averaging.

---

# 6. Why KNN

The KNN graph captures a basic form of homophily: people with similar behaviours, interests, economic context, decision style, or product relationship are more likely to exist in the same local social/consumer neighbourhood.

Distance can be weighted differently by product category.

For an AI product, important distance factors might include:

- AI trust;
- technology adoption;
- product need;
- price sensitivity;
- risk tolerance;
- lifestyle;
- income.

For another category, different dimensions can matter.

The system also adds a small fraction of **weak ties** so groups are not perfectly sealed. These can approximate online contacts, coworkers, acquaintances, or chance cross-group interactions.

# 7. Population Size Decision

Three primary modes were chosen.

## Small

```text
250 consumers
Base K = 10
```

Best for:

- debugging;
- portfolio demos;
- detailed visible dialogue;
- graph visualization;
- experimental behaviour rules.

## Standard — recommended

```text
1,000 consumers
Base K = 14
```

The normal simulation mode.

## Large

```text
5,000 consumers
Base K = 18
```

Best for:

- large diffusion experiments;
- segment analysis;
- Monte Carlo scenarios;
- research-style runs.

K grows slowly with population rather than linearly.

# 8. Conversation Capacity

Another important realism problem was identified: an agent might have many neighbours but cannot realistically talk to everyone at once.

The initial rule is:

```text
normal agent: 0–2 conversations in a round
rare/social agent: possibly 3 later
```

The current MVP cap is 2.

A second control was added after smoke-testing the scheduler: only about **20% of agents are selected as potential conversation initiators per round**, weighted by sociability and current product salience. Non-initiators can still receive a conversation. This prevents a five-minute round from turning into a near-universal discussion while preserving active social spread.

Influencers should not have thousands of individual conversations. Their reach is modeled as a broadcast/event mechanism later.

A candidate pair is scored from factors such as:

- similarity;
- relationship strength;
- product salience;
- sociability;
- topic relevance;
- cooldown/recent interaction;
- controlled seeded randomness.

Pairs are selected using capacity-aware matching.

---

# 9. Simulation Time

Initial decision:

> **1 round = 5 simulated minutes.**

This is simulated time, not wall-clock time.

A full round may execute faster or slower in real computing time.

The five-minute resolution is only a first abstraction and can later support other timescales.

---

# 10. Product Salience

A realism issue was identified: without an attention/salience mechanism, every person would discuss the product constantly.

Therefore agents need a product-salience value.

A product launch or advertisement can increase salience. Salience can then decay. New events such as:

- a friend recommendation;
- a viral review;
- a price reduction;
- product experience;
- an influencer post;

can raise salience again.

Low-salience consumers become less likely to start product conversations.

---

# 11. Language-Based Conversations

The project then moved from hidden numerical influence to **visible conversations**.

The map should show consumers talking to each other in natural language.

Initial language plan:

- English first;
- ordinary Indian English where relevant;
- avoid forced stereotypes/slang;
- later Hindi;
- later Hinglish;
- later additional Indian languages/code switching.

Typical conversation length:

```text
2–5 short utterances
```

The system does not need to generate a literal five-minute transcript just because one simulation round represents five minutes.

Example:

```text
Agent A:
₹999 feels expensive when there are free workout apps.

Agent B:
True, but this combines the plan, nutrition, and tracking. A trainer would cost much more.

Agent A:
That's fair. I'd still want to try it before paying monthly.
```

The user should later be able to click the active edge or conversation history and inspect:

- transcript;
- participating consumers;
- topics discussed;
- before state;
- after state;
- which beliefs changed;
- confidence changes;
- source trust.

# 12. Do Agents Need Separate LLM Instances?

No.

This became a critical cost/architecture decision.

Every consumer is a **persistent individual agent**, but an agent is not a separate running LLM process.

Agent individuality is stored in:

- traits;
- beliefs;
- confidence;
- knowledge;
- product salience;
- relationships;
- memories;
- conversation history;
- communication style;
- language profile.

A shared LLM service temporarily speaks as selected agents.

Conceptually:

```text
Agent A state ──┐
                ├── Shared LLM conversation engine
Agent B state ──┘
```

Then the shared model is reused for the next pair.

This allows thousands of consumers without thousands of permanent LLM instances.

---

# 13. DeepSeek Integration

DeepSeek Flash was selected as the primary intended conversational model because the conversation task is usually short, structured, and high-volume.

The LLM should do things such as:

- produce natural language;
- interpret a product message from an agent perspective;
- express objections;
- retell messages;
- produce qualitative explanation;
- return semantic topics/argument strengths.

The LLM should **not** decide final opinion or purchase intent directly.

The current implementation uses a provider abstraction so DeepSeek can later be replaced or supplemented.

A deterministic mock provider exists for development and testing.

---

# 14. Mathematical + Language Hybrid

The final conversation architecture is hybrid.

```text
Agent states
   ↓
conversation scheduler
   ↓
mathematical interaction OR LLM language interaction
   ↓
structured semantic evidence
   ↓
Python influence model
   ↓
new beliefs/state
```

This provides realism without turning the entire simulation into unpredictable prompt output.

# 15. Conversation Cost Modes

Population mode and language cost should be separate.

Planned dialogue modes:

## Economy

- most interactions numerical/semantic;
- important or visible conversations generated;
- low cost;
- suitable for large runs.

## Balanced — intended default

- selected meaningful conversations generated;
- important interactions generated;
- suitable for normal portfolio use.

## Full Dialogue

- every scheduled pair can receive generated dialogue;
- intended mainly for small/medium populations;
- higher cost/latency.

A future optimization may allow an interaction to run mathematically first and generate a transcript only if the user later clicks it. The transcript would render the already-recorded semantic event and would not rewrite history.

# 16. Multiple Conversations and the Last-Speaker Problem

A major logic bug was identified before implementation.

If a person talks to B, then C, then D and the code immediately updates state after each chat, final opinion depends on programming order.

Example:

```text
A starts +0.20
B pushes positive
C pushes negative
D pushes positive
```

Changing the array order could change the final answer. This is unacceptable as the default model.

Therefore ModelCouncil uses **synchronous round updates**.

At round start:

```text
freeze S(t)
```

All conversations use that same state snapshot.

Their effects are accumulated and one combined update is committed at the end:

```text
all conversations
   ↓
weighted evidence
   ↓
combined update
   ↓
S(t+1)
```

# 17. Topic-Specific Beliefs

One single `opinion = +0.5` is insufficient.

A person may simultaneously believe:

```text
usefulness      +0.80
quality         +0.45
price           -0.75
trust           +0.10
privacy         -0.30
```

A price conversation should mostly affect price/value. A privacy conversation should not automatically alter quality.

Overall opinion is derived from these dimensions.

Purchase intent is derived afterward from the relevant beliefs plus the consumer's need, income, price sensitivity, risk, etc.

Therefore a consumer can:

> love the idea but not buy it because it is too expensive.

This is a fundamental intended behaviour.

---

# 18. Aggregating Conflicting Opinions

Every incoming conversation produces topic evidence with factors such as:

- stance;
- argument strength;
- trust;
- relationship strength;
- similarity;
- speaker confidence;
- speaker knowledge;
- novelty.

Credibility and receptivity are calculated separately.

The system uses saturating influence so 50 people repeating a message are not automatically 50 times more powerful than one person.

Conflicting conversations can result in:

- small opinion movement;
- reduced confidence;
- higher uncertainty;
- no purchase decision change.

Agreement can strengthen confidence.

---

# 19. Bounded Confidence

Consumers should not automatically average completely opposing opinions.

Every agent has an effective acceptance radius influenced by traits such as stubbornness/openness/confidence.

A message close to an existing belief may be accepted strongly.

A very distant message may be weakly accepted or rejected.

Rare later behaviours can include reactance/backfire, but this should not be a default frequent event because it would artificially polarize the simulation.

---

# 20. Opinion Mutation and Message Propagation

The original KNN idea includes a key concept: **opinions travel while changing slightly**.

Example:

```text
A:
"The personalized plans save time."

B interprets:
"The app may be convenient."

B tells C:
"Apparently it saves you from searching for workouts."

C tells D:
"I heard it handles most of the planning for you."
```

The semantic core can persist while wording, certainty, and interpretation drift.

This supports later research around:

- word of mouth;
- rumours;
- semantic drift;
- misinformation;
- correction campaigns.

---

# 21. Facts vs Opinions

Another edge case was identified: an LLM could invent a product fact such as:

> "They have a 30-day free trial."

If this is allowed to become trusted truth, the entire synthetic market may respond to a hallucination.

Therefore facts and opinions must be distinguished.

Opinion:

> "₹999 feels expensive."

Product fact:

> "The free trial lasts 30 days."

Later the system should validate product facts against normalized product knowledge. Unsupported claims are rejected/regenerated unless the simulation explicitly enables rumour/misinformation mode.

---

# 22. Memory

Agents should eventually remember important interactions without sending their entire transcript history to the LLM every time.

A compact memory may store:

```text
source
round
topic
summary
impact
trust
confidence
```

Memory importance can depend on:

- effect magnitude;
- novelty;
- emotional intensity;
- source trust.

Weak interactions can be forgotten/compressed.

Memory decay and recency weighting are future behaviour modules.

---

# 23. Repetition vs Social Proof

Repeated arguments create two different phenomena.

If five people repeat exactly the same factual argument:

- information novelty decreases;
- social consensus signal increases.

These must not be treated as the same mechanism.

Future models should separately track argument novelty and social proof.

# 24. Influencers

Instead, future influencer mechanics use:

Reaction can depend on:

- follower affinity;

# 28. Web-First Decision

Originally the natural development route would have been a local Python/notebook prototype.

The project direction was then changed:

> **Everything should be web-viewable from the start.**

This does not mean business logic is placed in React. It means development begins with a browser surface and API while simulation remains a separate Python package.

Current architecture:

```text

      ↓
FastAPI API
      ↓
Simulation package
      ↓
LLM provider / data repositories
```

The first web screen accepts product-pitch text and simulation configuration.

Later input will include:

- product photos;

- PDF/product documents;

# 37. Definition of Success

A strong first complete ModelCouncil experience is:

1. user opens the website;
2. enters a product/pitch;
3. chooses population/dialogue configuration;
4. simulation generates a reproducible consumer population;
5. user sees initial consumer reaction;
6. KNN/weak-tie social graph is created;
7. realistic pairs converse over multiple rounds;
8. conversations produce topic evidence;
9. each round combines multiple opinions without order bias;
10. beliefs/confidence/opinion/purchase intent change;
11. the network map visibly evolves;
12. user can inspect conversations and reasons for state changes;
13. analytics explain which segments and social mechanisms drove the result;
14. results are clearly labelled as synthetic simulation outputs.

The product experience should feel like:

> **A controllable synthetic market laboratory, not a collection of chatbots.**
